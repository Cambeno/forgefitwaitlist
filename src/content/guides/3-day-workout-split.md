Three days a week is the most common real-world training schedule, and it's routinely treated as a compromise. It isn't. Some of the most effective strength programmes ever written run on exactly three sessions a week.

What changes at three days isn't whether you can build muscle: it's how you have to spend your time. With six sessions you can afford to dedicate one to arms. With three, every session has to earn its place.

## Your three options

### Full body ×3: best for most people

Every session trains every major movement pattern: squat, hinge, press, pull. Each muscle group gets three exposures a week.

**Best for:** beginners, anyone under two years of consistent training, and anyone whose main goal is getting stronger on the big lifts.

### Push/pull/legs ×1: most volume per muscle, least frequency

One push day, one pull day, one leg day. Each muscle gets trained hard once a week with high volume.

**Best for:** intermediate lifters who prefer longer, focused sessions and who've already built a strength base. See the full [PPL guide](/blog/push-pull-legs-split).

### Rotating upper/lower: the middle path

Upper, lower, upper one week; lower, upper, lower the next. Averages 1.5 sessions per muscle group per week.

**Best for:** lifters who want [upper/lower](/blog/upper-lower-split) structure but can't commit to four days.

For most people reading this, **full body ×3 is the right answer.** The rest of this guide covers it in full.

## The full-body 3-day programme

| Day | Session |
| --- | --- |
| Monday | Workout A |
| Wednesday | Workout B |
| Friday | Workout C |

## Progression

On a three-day programme you have room to run **straight linear progression** longer than you would on a higher-frequency split, because you're recovering fully between sessions.

**For your first few months:**

- Add 2.5 kg to upper-body lifts and 5 kg to lower-body lifts every time you hit all your reps.
- When you miss reps two sessions in a row, drop the weight by 10% and build back up.
- Expect this to last three to six months. It ends for everyone.

**After that, switch to double progression:**

1. Work within a rep range: say 5–8.
2. Add reps each session until you hit the top of the range on every set.
3. Then add weight and reset to the bottom of the range.

## Why three days can beat six

The three-day full-body lifter squats three times a week. The six-day PPL lifter squats twice, and often only heavy once.

Frequency drives skill. Squatting, benching and pulling are technical movements, and practising them three times a week makes you better at them faster. For anyone in their first couple of years, "better at the lift" is most of what "stronger" means.

> The trade-off is honest: full-body sessions cap how much volume any single muscle can take, because you're fatigued by the time you reach your accessories. That ceiling is why advanced lifters eventually move to four or six days. It is not a reason for a newer lifter to start there.

## Making the most of three days

**Never skip the main lift.** If time is short, cut accessories and finish the compound work. Two exercises done well beat six rushed.

**Rest properly on compounds.** Three minutes feels indulgent when you're watching the clock. Cutting rest to 90 seconds on squats reduces the weight you can handle, which reduces the stimulus: you save ten minutes and lose the point of the session.

**Add a fourth day only when you're stalling.** Not when you're bored. If linear progression is still working, a fourth day just adds fatigue.

**Eat and sleep like it matters.** Three sessions a week means each one has to count, and neither strength nor size shows up on insufficient food and six hours of sleep.

## Common mistakes

**Turning it into a bro split.** Three days is not chest day, back day, arms day. That's three muscle groups trained once a week and everything else neglected.

**Too many isolation exercises.** Every curl variation you add is a set you're taking from squats and rows. On three days, isolation work is a garnish.

**Running the same session three times.** You'll batter the same lifts at the same intensity and stall inside a month. Vary the lead lift.

**Not logging.** Linear progression is literally "add 2.5 kg to what you did last time". Without last time's number written down, there's nothing to add to.
