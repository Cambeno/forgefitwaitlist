Push/pull/legs is the most popular training split in the gym, and the one people get wrong most often. The idea is simple: group exercises by the movement pattern they share, so muscles that work together get trained together and rested together.

- **Push**: chest, shoulders, triceps. Everything where you press weight away from you.
- **Pull**: back, biceps, rear delts. Everything where you pull weight toward you.
- **Legs**: quads, hamstrings, glutes, calves.

The elegance is in what it avoids. Your triceps assist every pressing movement, so training chest on Monday and triceps on Tuesday means your triceps never get a real rest day. PPL solves that by putting all the pressing work in one session, then leaving those muscles alone until the rotation comes round again.

## Who PPL actually suits

PPL works best if you can train **6 days a week**, or if 3 days is your hard ceiling and you accept the trade-off.

It suits you if:

- You can commit to a fixed number of sessions and want the same session structure every time.
- You have been lifting for at least six months and can handle 15–20 sets in a session.
- You enjoy longer sessions with plenty of exercise variety.

Skip it if:

- **You can only train 4 or 5 days.** PPL doesn't divide into those numbers cleanly. Read the [upper/lower split](/blog/upper-lower-split) or the [4-day split](/blog/4-day-workout-split) instead.
- **You're brand new.** Frequency beats volume early on. A [3-day full-body split](/blog/3-day-workout-split) will build your squat and bench faster.
- **Your schedule is unpredictable.** The 6-day version punishes missed sessions: skip one and the whole rotation shifts.

## The 6-day PPL split

This is PPL as it's meant to be run. Each muscle group is trained twice a week, which research consistently shows beats once-weekly training for hypertrophy.

| Day | Session |
| --- | --- |
| Monday | Push A |
| Tuesday | Pull A |
| Wednesday | Legs A |
| Thursday | Push B |
| Friday | Pull B |
| Saturday | Legs B |
| Sunday | Rest |

The A and B sessions aren't identical. A-days lead with a heavy barbell lift in the 4–6 rep range; B-days lead with a different movement in the 8–12 range. That gives you both strength and hypertrophy stimulus without hammering the same pattern at the same intensity twice a week.

## The 3-day PPL split

One rotation a week. Each muscle group gets trained once, which is the honest weakness of this version, but three quality sessions you actually complete beat six you skip.

| Day | Session |
| --- | --- |
| Monday | Push |
| Wednesday | Pull |
| Friday | Legs |

Run the A-day templates above, but add one to two extra sets per exercise to compensate for the lower frequency. You're aiming for roughly 12–16 hard sets per muscle group per week, and with only one session to fit them in, each session gets denser.

If you have a fourth day available, don't force a fourth PPL session: add a full-body day focused on whichever lift you most want to improve.

## Progression: the part most people skip

A split is a schedule, not a programme. What actually drives growth is adding stress over time. Use **double progression**:

1. Pick your rep range: say 8–10.
2. Start at a weight where 8 reps is hard but clean.
3. Each session, try to add reps: 8, 8, 8 → 9, 8, 8 → 9, 9, 8 → and so on.
4. When you hit the top of the range on every set (10, 10, 10), add weight (2.5 kg for upper body, 5 kg for lower) and drop back to 8.
5. Repeat.

This is deliberately slow. That's the point. Adding 2.5 kg to your bench every three weeks is 40 kg a year, and almost nobody actually achieves that because they chase weight before they own the reps.

> Log the numbers or this doesn't work. Progressive overload requires knowing exactly what you did last time, not roughly, exactly. If your last bench session lives in your memory rather than a log, you are guessing.

## Deloads

Every 6–8 weeks, take a lighter week: same exercises, same sets, roughly 60% of your usual weight. You should leave every set feeling like you could have done five more reps.

Deloads feel like a waste of a week and they are not. Fatigue accumulates faster than you notice, and the week you spend backing off is the week your joints and central nervous system catch up with the work you've already done. Most people who "plateau" on PPL are simply too fatigued to express the strength they've built.

## Common PPL mistakes

**Too many exercises.** Six exercises is a session. Ten is a hobby. Once you pass about 20 hard sets, the extra work adds fatigue faster than it adds stimulus.

**Every set to failure.** Training to absolute failure on compound lifts wrecks your recovery for the rest of the week. Leave 1–2 reps in reserve on your main lifts, and save true failure for isolation work where the fatigue cost is low.

**Neglecting the pull day.** Push day is fun and visible. Pull day builds the back that holds your posture together and stabilises your bench. If you're skipping sessions, never let it be pull.

**No rear delt or upper back work.** Pressing volume without pulling volume rotates your shoulders forward over time. Face pulls and rear delt flyes are not optional accessories: they're insurance.

**Running it forever.** PPL is a good split, not a permanent one. After 12–16 weeks, change something: the exercises, the rep ranges, or the split itself.
